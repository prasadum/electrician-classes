Parts of the outside micrometer

This drawing follows the 0–25 mm instrument in the lab kit: blue C-frame, lever-type spindle lock on the frame boss, satin sleeve, knurled thimble, ratchet stop at the end and the C-spanner supplied in the box. Tap a number on the drawing or a card below.

How the parts work together

The micrometer converts rotation into a very small, accurately known straight-line movement. Inside the sleeve is a precision-ground screw that runs in a fixed nut. The spindle is the plain front end of that screw, so every turn of the thimble moves the spindle face towards or away from the anvil by exactly one pitch, 0.5 mm on this instrument.

The sleeve does not rotate. Its datum line and main scale tell you how many whole and half millimetres the spindle has travelled. The thimble is fixed to the screw, so it turns and slides along the sleeve; its bevelled edge acts as the pointer on the main scale, and its 50 divisions split each half-millimetre turn into fifty parts.

Why the ratchet and lock matter

A micrometer is accurate to 0.01 mm, but a hand turning the thimble can easily push hard enough to spring the frame or squash a soft wire by more than that. The ratchet stop slips once a fixed force is reached, so every reading, by every trainee, is taken under the same pressure.

The lock lever clamps the spindle. Use it to hold a reading while you lift the instrument off the job to read it, or to hold the spindle while setting zero with the C-spanner. Never force the thimble when the lock is on; it strains the screw.

Finding MSD, pitch, CSD and least count

Work through these four steps on the instrument before every practical. The magnified view below is the sleeve and thimble of the micrometer; use the buttons to turn the thimble and watch the edge travel along the main scale.

  1. Find the main scale division (MSD)Look along the datum line on the sleeve. Whole millimetre lines are above the line (numbered 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25) and half-millimetre lines are below it, staggered between them. Count the spaces between the 0 and 1 mm lines: there are two. So one main scale division = 1 mm ÷ 2 = 0.5 mm.The main scale runs 0–25 mm, which is 50 half-millimetre divisions along the sleeve.
  2. Find the pitch of the screwBring the thimble edge exactly onto a sleeve line with the thimble 0 on the datum. Turn the thimble one complete revolution. The edge moves forward by one main scale division. Pitch = distance moved ÷ number of rotations = 0.5 mm ÷ 1 = 0.5 mm.For a better measure, give 4 turns: the edge moves 2 mm, so pitch = 2 ÷ 4 = 0.5 mm. Averaging over several turns hides any small backlash.
  3. Count the circular scale divisions (CSD count)Count the equal divisions around the bevel of the thimble, or note the highest number before 0 and add one step (0, 5 … 45, then 0 again means 50 divisions). This thimble has 50 divisions.
  4. Calculate the least countThe least count is the distance the spindle moves when the thimble turns by one division.
    LC = Pitch ÷ No. of circular scale divisions= 0.5 mm ÷ 50 = 0.01 mm
    The least count is the smallest length the instrument can resolve. Any reading is a whole number of least counts.

Reading formula

Reading = MSR + (CSR × LC)MSR = main scale reading: the last sleeve line visible at the thimble edge (whole mm above, plus 0.5 if a lower line is showing).
CSR = circular scale reading: the thimble division that lines up with the datum line.
Corrected reading = Observed reading − (Zero error)Subtract the zero error with its sign. See tab 3.

Worked examples

Example 1: least count of the lab micrometer

Thimble edge moves 2.0 mm in 4 turns; thimble has 50 divisions.

  1. Pitch = 2.0 ÷ 4 = 0.5 mm
  2. LC = 0.5 ÷ 50 = 0.01 mm

Example 2: taking a reading

Last whole mm line visible: 7. A half-mm line below the datum is also visible. Thimble division 36 lines up with the datum.

  1. MSR = 7 + 0.5 = 7.5 mm
  2. CSR × LC = 36 × 0.01 = 0.36 mm
  3. Reading = 7.5 + 0.36 = 7.86 mm

Example 3: a 25-division thimble

Pitch 0.5 mm, 25 thimble divisions.

  1. LC = 0.5 ÷ 25 = 0.02 mm. Each thimble division is now worth 0.02 mm, so CSR 12 = 0.24 mm.

Zero error and its correction

Clean the faces, then close the spindle on the anvil using the ratchet until it clicks 2–3 times. With the faces touching, the thimble 0 should sit exactly on the datum line. If it does not, the instrument has a zero error.

No zero error. Thimble 0 coincides with the datum line and the sleeve 0 line is just visible.

Positive zero error. Thimble 0 is below the datum; division 3 is on the line. Zero error = +3 × 0.01 = +0.03 mm.

Negative zero error. Thimble 0 is above the datum; division 46 is on the line and the sleeve 0 is hidden. Zero error = −(50 − 46) × 0.01 = −0.04 mm.

Understanding the sign

A positive zero error means the instrument already reads something when there is nothing between the faces, so every reading is too large by that amount. It usually comes from the sleeve having been turned slightly, or from dust left on the faces.

A negative zero error means the thimble has gone past zero before the faces meet, so every reading is too small. Read it by counting how many divisions the 0 is above the datum: it equals N minus the division on the datum.

Positive: ZE = +(n × LC)n = division on the datum, thimble 0 below the line
Negative: ZE = −(N − n) × LCN = number of thimble divisions
Corrected = Observed − (ZE)

Subtracting a negative zero error adds it back. Always write the sign in your record.

Worked numericals

Positive zero error

Closed reading: division 3 on datum, 0 below it. Measured job: MSR 5.5 mm, CSR 28.

  1. ZE = +3 × 0.01 = +0.03 mm
  2. Observed = 5.5 + 28 × 0.01 = 5.78 mm
  3. Corrected = 5.78 − (+0.03) = 5.75 mm

Negative zero error

Closed reading: division 46 on datum, 0 above it. Measured wire: MSR 3.0 mm, CSR 12.

  1. ZE = −(50 − 46) × 0.01 = −0.04 mm
  2. Observed = 3.0 + 12 × 0.01 = 3.12 mm
  3. Corrected = 3.12 − (−0.04) = 3.16 mm

Removing it with the C-spanner

Clean the faces and close them with the ratchet. Lock the spindle. Fit the pin of the C-spanner into the small hole in the sleeve and turn the sleeve gently until its datum line meets the thimble 0. Unlock, open, re-close with the ratchet and check again. Errors larger than about 0.02 mm should be referred to the instructor; they are set by adjusting the thimble on the spindle.

Procedure: measuring with the micrometer

Each step is shown beside its animation. Press Next (or the → key) to move on; the animation repeats until you do.

All steps as a list
Prepare
  1. Select and inspect the instrumentConfirm the job size lies within 0–25 mm. Turn the thimble through its full range; it should move smoothly without play or tight spots. Check the lock lever is released.A 0–25 mm micrometer cannot measure 30 mm; a stiff screw gives false contact.
  2. Clean the measuring facesOpen the spindle a little. Wipe the anvil and spindle faces with clean, lint-free cloth, or close them lightly on a piece of clean paper and draw it out.A dust film of a few microns is already part of 0.01 mm.
  3. Find the least countMSD 0.5 mm, pitch 0.5 mm, 50 thimble divisions, LC = 0.01 mm (tab 2).
  4. Check the zero errorClose the spindle onto the anvil using the ratchet until it slips 2–3 clicks. Read the thimble at the datum and record the zero error with its sign.Check zero with the same ratchet pressure that you will use on the job.
Measure
  1. Open the spindle beyond the job sizeTurn the thimble anticlockwise (seen from the ratchet end) until the gap is a little larger than the job.
  2. Hold the micrometer and place the jobHold the frame in the palm of one hand, with thumb and forefinger free for the thimble. Place the job against the anvil, square to the spindle axis.A tilted job measures larger than its true size.
  3. Close with the thimble, finish with the ratchetTurn the thimble until the spindle just approaches the job, then turn only the ratchet until it slips 2–3 clicks.Turning the thimble after contact springs the frame and gives a low reading.
  4. Lock the spindleMove the lock lever to clamp the spindle, so the reading cannot change while you read or withdraw the instrument.
  5. Read the main scale (MSR)Note the last whole millimetre line visible above the datum, and add 0.5 mm if a half-millimetre line below the datum is visible past it.
  6. Read the circular scale (CSR)Note the thimble division that coincides with the datum line. Keep your eye square to the scale to avoid parallax.
  7. CalculateObserved = MSR + CSR × LC. Corrected = Observed − zero error.
  8. Repeat and take the meanUnlock, open, and measure again at three different positions (for a wire, also turn it through 90°). Record all trials and find the mean corrected reading.
Finish
  1. Unlock, open and storeRelease the lock, open the spindle, remove the job, wipe the instrument, and leave a small gap of about 0.5–1 mm between the faces. Put it back in its case with the spanner.Faces left touching can be strained by temperature changes and can cold-weld or rust together.

Precautions, in the order you meet them

Each precaution guards against a particular error. Learn the reason, not just the rule.

Before measuring
  1. Choose the right rangeUse a 0–25 mm micrometer only for jobs up to 25 mm.Never try to force a bigger job between the faces.
  2. Clean both measuring faces and the jobRemove dust, oil and burrs.Dirt adds its thickness to the reading.
  3. Check and record zero errorEvery time, before use, with ratchet pressure.Instruments get knocked; yesterday's zero is not today's.
  4. Allow the job to reach room temperatureDo not measure a job straight off a grinder or lathe.Steel expands about 0.012 mm per 100 mm per °C.
After measuring
  1. Release the lock before turning the thimbleTurning against the lock strains the screw and nut.
  2. Leave a gap and store in the caseAbout 0.5–1 mm between the faces, wiped and lightly oiled, away from magnets and vibration.
  3. Do not drop or knock the instrumentA blow can shift the zero or bend the frame.
During measuring
  1. Hold the micrometer by the frameDo not hold the sleeve or thimble in a closed fist for long.Body heat expands the frame and screw.
  2. Use the ratchet for final contact2–3 clicks only, the same for zero check and job.Equal pressure every time; the thimble alone gives uneven, excess force.
  3. Keep the job square to the spindleRock it gently to find the true minimum for a diameter.A tilted job reads oversize.
  4. Never measure a moving jobIt damages the faces and is dangerous on a running machine.
  5. Do not spin the instrument by the thimbleDo not twirl the frame to open or close it quickly.It wears the screw and loses calibration.
  6. Read with the eye square to the scaleViewing at an angle causes parallax error on the thimble.
  7. Take several readings and use the meanWires and jobs are seldom perfectly round or parallel.
